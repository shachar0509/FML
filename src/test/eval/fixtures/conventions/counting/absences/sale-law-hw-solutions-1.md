# Commercial Law — Homework 2, Model Answers (Part 1)

## Question 1 (20 points)
Noa bought a used car from a dealer. A week later the engine failed because of a defect that existed on the day of sale and that the dealer never mentioned. May Noa demand a repair or a refund?

**Issue.** Whether goods sold with a hidden defect entitle the buyer to remedies against the seller.

**Rule.** The Sale Law, 1968, s. 11 provides that the seller must deliver goods that conform to the contract, and goods with a defect that the buyer could not know of at the time of sale do not conform. Section 13 allows the buyer to demand repair or replacement, or to cancel, when the lack of conformity is not cured.

**Application.** The engine defect existed when the car was handed over, so the car did not conform within s. 11. Noa could not have discovered it on a normal inspection, and the dealer's silence means it was not part of the bargain. Noa complained within a week, so the remedies of s. 13 are open.

**Conclusion.** Noa may demand a repair, and if the dealer fails to cure it in reasonable time, may cancel the sale and receive a refund under s. 13.

## Question 2 (20 points)
Dan ordered 500 chairs for a wedding on 1 June. The seller promised delivery by 20 May and delivered on 28 May. Can Dan refuse the delivery?

**Issue.** Whether late delivery allows the buyer to refuse the goods.

**Rule.** Under the Sale Law, 1968, s. 7 the seller must deliver on the date fixed in the contract. Section 8 gives the buyer the right to cancel if the delay is a fundamental breach, that is, if the buyer would not have contracted had the breach been foreseen.

**Application.** The chairs were needed for 1 June. Delivery on 28 May was eight days late but still before the event, so Dan could use them. A delay that leaves the buyer able to use the goods for their purpose is not fundamental under s. 8, although it is a breach of s. 7.

**Conclusion.** Dan cannot cancel, but under s. 7 he may claim compensation for the harm caused by the eight-day delay.

## Question 3 (15 points)
Michal bought a laptop and noticed a faulty screen the day it was delivered. She wrote to the seller four months later. Is her complaint still in time?

**Issue.** Whether notice of a lack of conformity given four months after delivery is in time.

**Rule.** The Sale Law, 1968, s. 14 requires the buyer to notify the seller of the lack of conformity within a reasonable time after discovering it, and the buyer who does not loses the right to rely on it.

**Application.** Michal saw the fault on delivery day, so the reasonable time began then. A fault in a consumer product that is obvious on first use calls for notice within days or weeks, not months. Four months of silence is not reasonable and the seller was entitled to assume the laptop was accepted.

**Conclusion.** Under s. 14 Michal's complaint came too late and she loses the remedies for the lack of conformity.

## Question 4 (15 points)
Yael told her friend Ron on the phone, "I'll sell you my bicycle for 300 shekels." Ron answered, "Deal." The next day Yael changed her mind. Is she bound?

**Issue.** Whether a binding contract was formed by the phone call.

**Rule.** The sale of a bicycle falls under the Sale Law as the background law for sales of goods, but whether a contract exists is decided by the general principles of contract formation: an offer that is definite and shows intent to be bound, and an unconditional acceptance communicated to the offeror.

**Application.** Yael's words state the goods and the price and show intent to be bound, so they are an offer. Ron's "Deal" accepted it without change, and it was communicated during the call. Nothing in the call left a term open, and the fact that the parties are friends does not remove the intention to create legal relations for a sale for money.

**Conclusion.** A contract was formed in the call and Yael is bound.

## Question 5 (15 points)
Amir bought a painting for a high price at a gallery because the gallery said it was by a famous painter. It later turned out to be by a pupil. Can Amir cancel?

**Issue.** Whether a mistake about the authorship of a painting lets the buyer cancel the sale.

**Rule.** Although the sale is governed by the Sale Law, the question is not about delivery, conformity or notice. It turns on the doctrine of mistake induced by the other side's statement, as applied in the leading case of the Supreme Court in the Cohen matter: a buyer who contracted because of a representation about an essential quality of the item may cancel.

**Application.** The gallery itself stated that the painter was famous, and authorship determined the price. The statement was made before the sale and Amir relied on it. Authorship is an essential quality of an artwork, as the court held in the Cohen matter on similar facts.

**Conclusion.** Amir may cancel the sale and recover the price.
