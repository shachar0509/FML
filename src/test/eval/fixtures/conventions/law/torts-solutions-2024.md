## Question 1 (25 points)
Ronit took her large dog for a walk in a public park. The dog slipped its lead and bit a jogger, Tamar, on the calf. Tamar needed stitches and missed three weeks of work. Ronit says the dog had never bitten anyone before. Is Ronit liable?

Summary: Ronit is strictly liable as the keeper of the dog, and her clean record does not help her.

### Issue
Is the keeper of a dog liable for a bite even though the dog had no history of aggression, and does Tamar have to prove carelessness?

### Rule
Section 41 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version] provides that the keeper of an animal is liable for damage it causes if the animal is of a kind that is dangerous by nature, or if the keeper knew that the animal was dangerous. For an animal of a kind that is not dangerous by nature, liability follows when the keeper knew or should have known of its tendency to harm. Where section 41 applies, the plaintiff does not have to show a breach of the duty of care in section 35. Defences are that the plaintiff provoked the animal or willingly took the risk.

---page---

### Application
Keeper. Ronit owns the dog and had it on a lead in the park, so she is its keeper at the time of the bite.

Type of animal. A large dog is not dangerous by nature in the way a wild animal is. The question under section 41 is therefore knowledge. Ronit says the dog never bit before. But the section speaks of what the keeper knew or should have known, not only of past bites. The facts say the dog was large and that it slipped its lead. A keeper of a large dog in a public park, among joggers and children, should know that sudden movements by passers-by can trigger a chase or a bite, and should take care that the lead and the harness are secure. That a dog was able to slip its lead indicates that the equipment was not secure, which a keeper should have known by looking at it.

Causation and damage. The bite caused the wound, the stitches and the three weeks of lost work. All are direct and foreseeable consequences.

Defences. There is no evidence that Tamar provoked the dog. A jogger running past a dog is not provocation; she was in a place where she was entitled to be and was doing nothing unusual. She did not voluntarily take the risk of being bitten.

Ronit will argue that she took all reasonable precautions, namely a lead. But the strict element of section 41 means that reasonable care by the keeper is not an answer if the knowledge requirement is met, and the lead in this case failed.

### Conclusion
Ronit is liable to Tamar under section 41 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance. She is the keeper, she should have known that her large dog needed a secure restraint in a busy park, and no defence applies. Tamar recovers her medical costs, lost wages and compensation for pain and suffering.

## Question 2 (25 points)
Gil rode his motorcycle without a helmet and was hit by a car that Dina drove through a red light. Gil suffered a head injury. His damages are assessed at 400,000. Experts agree that a helmet would have made the injury considerably less serious. How much will Gil recover?

Summary: Dina is liable in full for the collision, but Gil's damages are reduced because he rode without a helmet.

### Issue
Does Gil's decision not to wear a helmet reduce the damages he can recover from Dina, and if so, by how much?

### Rule
Dina's liability rests on negligence under section 35 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version]. Section 68 of the Ordinance provides that where damage was caused partly by the plaintiff's own fault and partly by the fault of the defendant, the damages are reduced to the extent that the court finds just and equitable, having regard to each party's share in the responsibility for the damage. The question is not whether Gil caused the accident but whether his carelessness contributed to the damage.

### Application
Dina's liability. Driving through a red light is plainly what no reasonable driver would do. Under section 35 she breached her duty, and the collision was caused by her breach. Gil does not need to establish more than that.

Gil's fault. Riding without a helmet did not cause the collision. Section 68, however, looks to the damage and not to the accident. The experts agree that a helmet would have made the head injury considerably less serious. A reasonable motorcyclist would have worn one, and wearing one costs almost nothing. His omission therefore contributed to the extent of the harm, and he was at fault in the sense of the section.

Apportionment. The court weighs the culpability of each party and the causal importance of each failing. Dina's conduct was the cause of the event, and it was a serious fault; she put a rider in danger by driving through a red light. Gil's omission did not make the accident happen, but it made its consequences much worse. A reduction of about 25 percent for Gil's share is a fair evaluation, because the helmet would have reduced severity considerably but not prevented any injury at all.

Calculation. A reduction of 25 percent from 400,000 is 100,000, so Gil recovers 300,000.

### Conclusion
Dina is liable in negligence, and Gil's damages are reduced under section 68 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance for his failure to wear a helmet. On a 25 percent reduction, he recovers 300,000.

## Question 3 (25 points)
Hadas, a first-time buyer, asked an accountant, Eli Haddad, whether a small bakery was a sound purchase. Haddad had not been engaged by Hadas and was at a dinner party when he said, casually, that the bakery's books looked healthy. He had skimmed them once. Hadas bought the bakery and found that it was close to insolvent. She sues Haddad for her loss. Discuss.

Summary: Haddad is probably not liable, because a casual remark at a dinner party does not create a duty of care for a purely financial loss.

### Issue
Does an accountant owe a duty of care for the accuracy of a casual statement of opinion, where the only loss is financial and the listener acted on the statement?

### Rule
The Civil Wrongs Ordinance is the framework within which negligent statements are litigated, but the scope of the duty for a statement is a product of case law. In Barak v. Haddad the court held that a person who gives advice or information owes a duty of care for it only if two conditions are satisfied: he knew that the recipient would rely on it for a specific purpose, and in the circumstances it was reasonable for the recipient to rely on it. Casual remarks made in a social setting do not ordinarily meet the second condition.

### Application
The first condition. Haddad knew that Hadas was considering buying a bakery, because she asked him about it. He may be taken to have known that his comment would be used in her decision. This satisfies the first condition, though barely, since Haddad had no engagement and was not paid.

The second condition. Reliance has to be reasonable. Hadas knew that Haddad had only skimmed the books once, as the facts tell us, and that he spoke at a dinner party. In Barak v. Haddad the court emphasised that a professional who speaks in an informal setting, without papers, a fee or a disclaimer, is not understood to be giving a professional opinion. A reasonable purchaser of a business would commission a due diligence review, and an offhand remark from a guest cannot replace it. Hadas's reliance falls short of reasonable.

Haddad's expertise does not alter the analysis. Expertise matters where the speaker assumes a professional role. Here he did not. Hadas could have chosen to engage him, and she did not.

Policy. If every casual remark by a professional created a duty, professionals would stop speaking in any context outside their engagements, which would be an undesirable result.

### Conclusion
Haddad owed Hadas no duty of care for his remark. His statement was casual, she knew that he had barely looked at the books, and her reliance was not reasonable. Her claim in negligence fails.

## Question 4 (25 points)
Lior runs a bakery whose ovens and extractor fans begin work at 3 a.m. each morning. Michal lives in the flat above and says that the noise and vibration wake her and her two children every night. She sues for an injunction and damages. Lior says that bakeries have always existed in the neighbourhood. Discuss.

Summary: Michal has a good claim in private nuisance, because the noise is a substantial and unreasonable interference at night.

### Issue
Is the bakery's night-time noise a private nuisance to Michal, and does the age of the neighbourhood's bakeries give Lior a defence?

### Rule
Section 44 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version] makes it a nuisance for a person to do anything on his land that unreasonably interferes with the use or enjoyment of neighbouring land. Whether an interference is unreasonable depends on its severity, duration and timing, the character of the locality, and the cost and ease of avoiding it. Section 44 does not require proof of carelessness.

### Application
Interference. Noise and vibration at 3 a.m. which wake a household every night is a severe interference with the use and enjoyment of a home. Sleep is the most basic part of enjoyment of a dwelling, and the interference is not occasional but nightly.

Reasonableness. The timing is critical. A bakery working at midday in a commercial street might make comparable noise without any complaint. Here the noise is at the hour when residents are asleep, and it continues day after day. The locality has a mixed character, with homes above shops, and a resident in such a locality accepts a degree of commercial noise; but not noise of this kind at that hour.

Lior's defence. The fact that bakeries have always existed in the neighbourhood does not make the nuisance reasonable. Long usage of a locality is a factor in assessing its character, not a licence. It would be a different matter if Michal had moved in after the bakery was working, but even then the existence of a nuisance would not be excluded. The facts do not say when each of them arrived, and this would need to be checked.

Avoidance. Fitting sound insulation, mounting the fans on dampers and delaying the heaviest work until 5 a.m. are measures of moderate cost. A defendant who can avoid the interference cheaply has less claim to say that it is reasonable.

Remedy. An injunction restricting the hours of the loudest equipment is appropriate, together with damages for the disturbance to date.

### Conclusion
The night-time operation of the bakery is a private nuisance under section 44 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance, and Michal's claim succeeds. The court should restrict the hours of operation of the noisiest equipment and award her damages for the interference she has suffered so far. **Therefore, the claim succeeds.**
