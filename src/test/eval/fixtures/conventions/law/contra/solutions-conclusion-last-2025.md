## Question 1 (25 points)
Amit was cycling along a shared path at dusk with no lights. Rina, walking her toddler, stepped out of the pedestrian lane to retrieve a ball and was knocked down by Amit. She broke her collarbone. Amit says Rina should have watched where she was going. Who is liable, and for how much?

### Issue
Is Amit liable in negligence for the collision, and does Rina's own conduct reduce what she recovers?

### Rule
Negligence is defined in section 35 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version]: doing what a reasonable person would not have done, or failing to do what a reasonable person would have done. Section 68 of the Ordinance allows the court to reduce damages where the plaintiff's own fault contributed to the damage, to the extent the court finds just and equitable.

---page---

### Application
Duty and breach. A cyclist on a shared path owes a duty to pedestrians, who are obviously within the class of people he must foresee will be affected by his conduct. Cycling at dusk without lights deprives other path users of any warning and also deprives the cyclist of the ability to see; a reasonable cyclist would have used lights, or slowed to walking pace. Amit breached section 35.

Causation. The collision caused the fracture, and the absence of lights explains why neither party avoided the other.

Contributory fault. Rina stepped out of her lane. A path of this kind is, however, used by people with children, and a parent chasing a ball is behaving in a way that a reasonable cyclist must anticipate. Her fault is small: perhaps 10 percent, for not looking before stepping out. Amit's fault is the main one.

Damages. If the court assesses Rina's total damages at 80,000, the reduction under section 68 is 8,000 and she recovers 72,000.

### Conclusion
Amit is liable in negligence under section 35 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance, and Rina's damages are reduced by about 10 percent under section 68 for her own small share of the fault. She recovers roughly 72,000 on the figures assumed.

## Question 2 (25 points)
Yaara joined a gym on a two-year plan, signing the agreement over the phone. Three days later she changed her mind and asked to cancel. The gym says that membership agreements cannot be cancelled once signed. Is the gym right?

### Issue
Can Yaara cancel a membership agreement made by telephone within a few days, despite the gym's refusal?

### Rule
Section 14 of the Consumer Protection Law, 1981 allows a consumer who concluded a distance sale, including one made by telephone, to cancel it within fourteen days of the agreement, or of receiving the written details of the transaction if later. The right cannot be waived by a term in the standard agreement. The supplier may retain a small cancellation fee, fixed by the Law as a percentage of the price, if the consumer has already used the service.

### Application
Distance sale. The agreement was concluded by phone, and neither party was present in the same place. That makes it a distance sale for the purposes of section 14.

Time. Yaara asked to cancel three days after the agreement, well within the fourteen-day window.

The gym's term. The gym relies on a clause that membership cannot be cancelled. A clause that purports to remove a right given by section 14 is of no effect, since the right cannot be waived by a standard term. The gym's argument therefore fails.

Fees. If Yaara has attended sessions in the three days, the gym may retain the statutory fee, but no more. It may not hold her to the two-year plan.

### Conclusion
The gym is wrong. Yaara cancelled within the fourteen days permitted by section 14 of the Consumer Protection Law, and the clause that purports to forbid cancellation has no effect. She may end the agreement, subject only to the statutory fee.

## Question 3 (25 points)
A pet shop owner, Boaz, kept a large parrot in an open cage by the doorway. The parrot flew at a customer, Shir, and scratched her eye. Boaz knew that the parrot had attacked another customer a month earlier. What is the claim?

### Issue
Is Boaz liable for the damage caused by the parrot, given his knowledge of its earlier attack?

### Rule
Section 41 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version] makes the keeper of an animal liable for the damage it causes where the keeper knew that the animal was dangerous. The plaintiff need not prove carelessness. Provocation by the plaintiff or voluntary assumption of the risk are defences.

### Application
Keeper. Boaz owns the parrot and keeps it in his shop, so he is its keeper.

Knowledge. He knew that the parrot attacked a customer a month earlier. That is actual knowledge of a tendency to harm, which is the central requirement of section 41, and it was combined with a decision to leave the parrot in an open cage at the entrance, where every customer passes.

Defences. Shir did nothing to provoke the bird. Entering a shop is not an assumption of the risk of being attacked by its livestock.

Damage. The eye injury is a direct result of the attack. The extent of the damage is a matter for the medical evidence, but liability does not depend on it.

### Conclusion
Boaz is liable to Shir under section 41 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance. He knew of the parrot's tendency to attack, kept it where customers pass, and has no defence. Shir recovers for her eye injury.
