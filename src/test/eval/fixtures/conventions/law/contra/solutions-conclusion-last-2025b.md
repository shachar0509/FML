# Torts and Contracts — Practice Solutions, Set B (2025)
Additional example solutions for revision. Answers are given in full.

## Question 1 (25 points)
Nadav ordered 500 ceramic tiles from Bayit Supplies for a restaurant opening on 1 June. Bayit promised delivery by 15 May but delivered on 28 May. Nadav, who had hired a tiler at a fixed fee, had to postpone the opening by ten days and lost profits of 30,000. Bayit says that the delay was a minor breach. Can Nadav recover?

### Issue
Is the delay a breach for which Nadav may claim compensation, and is the lost profit recoverable?

### Rule
Section 28 of the Sale of Goods Law, 1968 allows a buyer to claim compensation for damage caused by the seller's breach, including delay in delivery, in addition to or in place of cancelling the contract. The damage recoverable is the damage that the seller foresaw or should have foreseen, when the contract was made, as a likely consequence of a breach. The buyer must take reasonable steps to reduce the loss.

### Application
Breach. Delivery was due on 15 May and took place on 28 May, a delay of thirteen days. The delivery date was a term of the contract, and whether or not the delay would justify cancellation, it is plainly a breach that entitles the buyer to compensation.

Foreseeability. Bayit knew that the tiles were for a restaurant with an opening date of 1 June, since that was stated when the order was placed. A seller who knows of a fixed opening should foresee that late delivery will delay the opening and that profit will be lost. The loss is not remote.

Mitigation. Nadav had a tiler on a fixed fee and could not have brought in a second crew in less than the time that remained. We are not told of any cheaper alternative, such as buying a few tiles locally to finish the main dining area, and Bayit may argue that Nadav should have tried. If the court accepts that argument, the lost profit would be reduced to what could not have been avoided.

Quantum. Lost profits of 30,000 for a ten-day delay is a calculation that needs proof, usually through the restaurant's projections or comparable trading figures. The figure must be net profit, not revenue.

### Conclusion
Bayit is in breach of its delivery obligation, and Nadav may claim compensation under section 28 of the Sale of Goods Law for the foreseeable lost profit, subject to proof of the amount and to any failure to mitigate. Nadav's claim succeeds in principle.
