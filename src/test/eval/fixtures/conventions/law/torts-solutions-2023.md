## Question 1 (30 points)
Noa was shopping at Marmelada Market when she slipped on spilled yogurt in aisle 6 and fractured her wrist. There was no warning sign, the aisle was last inspected ninety minutes earlier, and two employees had walked past the spill without cleaning it. Noa sues the market in negligence. Will she succeed?

### Issue
The question is whether the market is liable to Noa in negligence: did it owe her a duty of care, did it breach that duty by leaving a known hazard on the floor, and was her fracture caused by that breach?

### Rule
Negligence is defined in section 35 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version]: a person is negligent if he does an act that a reasonable person in the same circumstances would not have done, or fails to do an act that a reasonable person would have done. The duty to take care is set out in section 36 of the Ordinance, which extends it to anyone who a reasonable person should have foreseen would be affected by the conduct or omission in the circumstances of the case. Three elements follow: a duty, a breach measured against the reasonable person, and damage caused by the breach.

### Application
Duty. A supermarket invites the public onto its premises and controls the condition of its floors. A customer walking down an aisle is the paradigm of a person who a reasonable operator must foresee will be affected by a hazard in that aisle. The duty under section 36 is therefore plain, and the market has not argued otherwise.

Breach. The standard under section 35 is what a reasonable supermarket operator would have done. Such an operator knows that liquids spill in a food aisle, that customers look at shelves and not at the floor, and that a spill is cheap to remove and expensive to leave. Two employees walked past the spill. That fact matters more than the ninety-minute gap between inspections, because it shows actual knowledge on the part of the market's own staff, and a reasonable operator would have either cleaned the spill at once or cordoned it off. The absence of a sign is a further omission. The market might say that an inspection every ninety minutes is a reasonable system; but a system is only as good as the response of the people running it, and here the response failed.

Causation. Noa slipped on the yogurt and fell on her outstretched hand. But for the spill, she would not have fallen. A fractured wrist is a typical and foreseeable injury from a fall on a hard floor, so there is no difficulty with remoteness.

Defences. Noa's own conduct is not alleged to be careless: the spill was white on a pale floor and a customer reading labels could not be expected to notice it. There is no basis for a reduction.

### Conclusion
The market owed Noa a duty of care, breached it by leaving a spill that its own employees had seen, and that breach caused the fracture. Noa's claim in negligence succeeds, and she is entitled to damages for her injury and losses.

## Question 2 (30 points)
Yossi worked at a metal workshop and lost three fingers when his hand was drawn into a stamping press. The press had no guard. Yossi had been shown the press on his first day and had been told it was dangerous. The Workplace Safety Ordinance, 1954 applies to the workshop. Can Yossi base a claim on the workshop's breach of that Ordinance?

### Issue
Does breach of a safety enactment give Yossi a civil claim, and if so, are the conditions of that claim satisfied on these facts?

### Rule
Section 63 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version] creates the tort of breach of statutory duty. A person who fails to comply with an enactment that imposes a duty on him is liable if four conditions are met: the enactment was intended for the benefit or protection of a person like the plaintiff; the plaintiff was harmed by the breach; the harm is of the kind the enactment was intended to prevent; and, on a proper construction of the enactment, it was not intended to exclude a civil remedy. Section 9 of the Workplace Safety Ordinance, 1954 provides that every stamping or pressing machine with a moving part that can reach an operator must be fitted with a fixed guard while the machine is in use.

### Application
Duty imposed. Section 9 of the Workplace Safety Ordinance addresses employers and occupiers in so many words. It leaves no discretion: the guard must be fitted whenever the machine runs.

Protected class. The purpose of section 9 is to protect the people who operate and work near the machine. Yossi was the operator. He is within the class for whose benefit the duty was created.

Breach. The press had no guard. The workshop therefore failed to do what section 9 required, and the failure is established without the need to ask what a reasonable employer would have done. That is the practical advantage of section 63 over section 35: the standard of care is fixed by the legislature.

Kind of harm. Section 9 is aimed at exactly one danger, a hand reaching a moving part. Yossi lost fingers in that way. The harm is within the mischief the enactment was designed to prevent.

No exclusion of a civil remedy. Nothing in the Workplace Safety Ordinance suggests that criminal penalties are the only sanction. The enactment protects a vulnerable class against grave bodily injury; denying that class damages would remove much of the incentive to comply.

The workshop will say that Yossi had been warned. A warning does not discharge a duty to guard: the very purpose of a fixed guard is to protect a worker who is tired, distracted or hurried. At most the warning might support an argument of contributory fault, but a worker is not expected to compensate for the employer's failure to do what the law required.

### Conclusion
Yossi may base his claim on section 63 of the Civil Wrongs Ordinance together with the breach of section 9 of the Workplace Safety Ordinance. All four conditions are met, and the workshop is liable for the loss of his fingers, subject to at most a modest reduction for any carelessness on his part.

## Question 3 (25 points)
Arbel Builders negligently cut an underground power cable while excavating on a building site. The Dagan Dairy factory next door lost electricity for two days. It lost production worth 180,000, but its premises and equipment were not damaged. Dagan Dairy sues Arbel Builders in negligence for the lost production. Discuss.

### Issue
Does a person who negligently causes a power failure owe a duty of care to a neighbour whose only loss is financial, with no physical damage to its property?

### Rule
The Civil Wrongs Ordinance states the duty of care in general terms, and its text says nothing about loss that is purely economic. How far the duty reaches for such loss has been settled by the courts. In Levi v. Cohen the court held that a defendant is not liable for pure economic loss that flows from damage to a third party's property, unless there was a special relationship between the parties: the defendant knew that the plaintiff in particular would rely on his care, or the defendant had assumed responsibility towards that plaintiff. The reasoning was a concern about indeterminate liability, since one careless act can produce a very large number of claims by people who suffer a loss of profit.

### Application
Dagan Dairy suffered no damage to its premises or equipment. The cable belonged to the utility, and the damage to that cable is the only physical damage in the story. What Dagan Dairy lost is production, and the loss is economic in the sense that matters under Levi v. Cohen.

The next question is whether there was a special relationship. Arbel Builders did not know the dairy was dependent on that cable, did not deal with it and gave it no assurance. It was an unwitting neighbour, one of many businesses served by the same line. If the dairy could recover, so could every house, shop and office on the circuit, and Arbel Builders would face liability out of all proportion to a momentary lapse. This is the situation that the ruling in Levi v. Cohen was concerned with.

The dairy may argue that its loss was entirely foreseeable, since a factory needs power. Foreseeability alone does not settle the matter; the court held that it is a necessary but not a sufficient condition when the loss is purely financial. The dairy may also say it was uniquely exposed because it ran refrigerated stock. That does not create a relationship with Arbel Builders, which did not know it. The dairy had its own means of protecting itself, through a standby generator or business interruption insurance, and the law prefers to leave that kind of risk with the party best placed to insure.

### Conclusion
Arbel Builders owes Dagan Dairy no duty of care for its lost production. The loss is purely economic, there is no special relationship between the parties, and the policy against indeterminate liability applies. The claim fails, although the utility itself may recover for the damage to its cable.
