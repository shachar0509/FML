## Question 1 (20 points)
Define "consideration" in the law of contracts, explain what function it performs, and explain whether the courts require it to be adequate.

### Issue
The question asks what consideration is, why the law of contract asks for it, and whether the law inquires into the value of what is exchanged.

### Rule
Consideration is something of value that each party gives or promises in exchange for the other party's promise. It can be a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee, and the two are normally the same event seen from different sides. The doctrine's classic function is to distinguish a bargain, which the law enforces, from a gratuitous promise, which it generally does not. The courts have long held that consideration must be sufficient, in the sense that it has some value recognised by law, but need not be adequate, in the sense of being equal in market value to the promise. The leading ruling for the second proposition is Rosen v. Mizrahi, where the court refused to set aside a sale of a vineyard for a price far below its value, saying that the court does not make bargains for the parties.

---page---

### Application
The doctrine can be explained through three examples.

First, a promise by a grandfather to give his grandson a car, with nothing requested in return. There is no bargain, no consideration moves from the grandson, and the promise is a gift. If the grandfather changes his mind before delivery, the grandson has no contractual claim.

Second, a promise by the grandfather to give the car if the grandson passes his driving test. Passing the test is a detriment to the grandson in the sense that it is an act requested by the promisor in exchange for the promise, and a conditional promise of this kind is generally enforceable as a unilateral contract once the grandson performs. Here consideration is present because the act was requested.

Third, the sale of a rare coin for one shekel. The price is grossly inadequate, but it is something of value in law, and under Rosen v. Mizrahi the court will not inquire into the fairness of the bargain. A shekel is sufficient. Where the inadequacy is extreme the court might see in it evidence of fraud or undue pressure, and then deal with the case on those grounds and not on a lack of consideration.

Past consideration is also worth mentioning, since it marks the edge of the concept. A promise to pay for work that has already been completed, made after the fact and not requested in advance, is generally not supported by consideration because the work was not given in exchange for the promise. The law has developed exceptions, but they lie beyond this question.

The doctrine's critics argue that the requirement is both over- and under-inclusive: it denies enforcement to serious gratuitous promises made in writing, and it enforces trivial exchanges. Defenders answer that it supplies a simple test for seriousness and that its practical results are tolerable.

### Conclusion
Consideration is value given or promised in exchange for a promise, and its function is to separate enforceable bargains from gifts. The courts require it to be sufficient and not adequate, so a nominal but real price is enough, as the ruling in Rosen v. Mizrahi shows.

## Question 2 (30 points)
Orly bought a laptop from Compuline for 4,200 for use in her design business. The screen began to flicker after two weeks, and the fault became worse until it was unusable. Orly was abroad, and she informed Compuline of the problem only five months after the purchase. Compuline refuses to accept the laptop back. Orly wants to cancel the contract and recover her price. Discuss.

### Issue
Was the laptop non-conforming, did Orly give notice in time, and if she did not, has she lost the right to cancel the contract?

### Rule
Section 11 of the Sale of Goods Law, 1968 provides that the seller must deliver goods that conform to the contract in quality, and goods do not conform if they are not fit for the purposes for which goods of that kind are ordinarily used. Section 15 provides that a buyer loses the right to rely on a lack of conformity if he does not give the seller notice that specifies its nature within a reasonable time after he discovered it or ought to have discovered it. Section 28 of the Law gives a buyer the right to cancel the contract and be restored to his position where the lack of conformity amounts to a fundamental breach.

### Application
Non-conformity. A laptop whose screen flickers and then fails cannot be used for the purposes for which laptops are ordinarily used. A defect that shows itself within two weeks suggests that it was present at delivery, since the fault did not arise from any misuse. Section 11 is therefore engaged, and the defect is serious enough to amount to a fundamental breach, because it deprives the buyer of substantially what she contracted to receive.

Notice. The difficulty lies in section 15. Orly discovered the problem in the second week but informed Compuline only after five months. The test is a reasonable time from when the defect was discovered or should have been. She was abroad, which is relevant, but it is not decisive: a message to the seller could have been sent from any country, and a business buyer with a design practice is expected to act promptly. The worsening of the fault over time makes late notice worse, since Compuline lost the chance of examining the laptop while the cause was still clear, and of repairing or replacing it before the loss grew. Five months is, in the circumstances, not a reasonable time. Orly may say that Compuline was not prejudiced, but section 15 does not make prejudice a condition.

Consequence. Since notice was not given in time, Orly loses the right to rely on the lack of conformity, and the right of cancellation in section 28 is not available to her. It does not matter that the defect was real.

Possible arguments for Orly. She might claim that Compuline's own warranty promised a repair, which could be a separate basis. The facts do not mention a warranty, and without one the claim rests on the Law alone.

### Conclusion
The laptop did not conform to the contract under section 11 of the Sale of Goods Law, but Orly gave notice only after five months and so lost the right to rely on the defect under section 15. She cannot cancel the contract under section 28 of the Law. **Therefore, the claim fails.**

## Question 3 (25 points)
Samir owns a roof-repair business. In March he emailed a property manager, Iris, with a quotation for replacing the roof of a warehouse for 60,000, valid "until the end of the month". Iris did not reply. In April Samir's crew arrived at the warehouse, Iris saw them begin work, and said nothing. Samir completed the work and demanded 60,000. Iris says she never accepted. Is there a contract?

### Issue
Can a contract be formed by conduct and silence where the offeree never expressed acceptance in words, and the offer was by its terms limited to the end of March?

### Rule
The Sale of Goods Law, 1968 governs contracts for the sale of goods, but this is a contract for work and services on a building, and the question is one of ordinary contractual intent. The central issue is whether the parties, taken objectively, intended to create a legally binding agreement on the terms offered. Intention is judged by what a reasonable person in the position of each party would understand from the other's words and conduct. Silence alone is not acceptance, but silence combined with conduct from which assent can reasonably be inferred may be. A late acceptance operates as a new offer, which the original offeror may accept in turn.

### Application
The email. Samir's quotation of 60,000 stated a price, a job and a time limit, and it was addressed to a person with authority to engage contractors. It was sufficiently definite to amount to an offer rather than an invitation to treat. It lapsed by its own terms at the end of March.

Silence in March. Iris did not reply during March. No acceptance was made within the period, since silence is not acceptance.

April. When Samir's crew arrived, they began performance on the strength of the quotation. If their start is treated as a fresh offer, namely to do the work at the quoted price, it was open to Iris to refuse it. She watched the work begin and said nothing. Here the combination matters. A property manager who sees contractors start on her building and knows the price they expect knows that they are working in the expectation of payment, and she has an easy and obvious means of stopping them. Her silence while the crew carried out the work can reasonably be read as accepting the new offer by conduct. A reasonable person in Samir's position would have understood that Iris had agreed. The relevant intention is objective: Iris's private view that she never accepted does not displace the impression her behaviour created.

Counter-argument. Iris may say that she thought the work had been arranged by the owner. This is possible, but she does not say that she told Samir so, and a belief that was never communicated has no effect on what the other party reasonably understood.

Price. The price proposed was 60,000, and no other price was discussed. If the court found no contract, Samir would still have a claim for the reasonable value of work that Iris accepted, so the practical difference is small.

### Conclusion
A contract was formed by conduct in April, on the terms of Samir's quotation, because Iris's silence while the work proceeded was reasonably understood as acceptance. Samir can recover the 60,000.
